# Sorter — design and methodology

This document describes what Sorter does, the method it uses, the guarantees
that method provides, and how those guarantees were verified. It is written to
serve two readers: someone picking the tool up for the first time, and someone
who needs to describe the allocation procedure in a methods section.

---

## 1. The problem

When work is scored by human raters and you intend to measure agreement between
them, *how raters are allocated to items is part of the measurement*. Three
requirements have to hold simultaneously.

**1. Double scoring.** Every item is scored by two different raters. This is
what makes an agreement statistic computable at the item level.

**2. Balanced workload.** Raters carry comparable numbers of items. Beyond
fairness, an imbalance biases any rater-level effect estimate: a rater who sees
three times as many items contributes three times as much to pooled statistics
and their idiosyncrasies dominate.

**3. Dispersed pairings.** Across the set, each rater is paired with as many
*different* partners as possible.

The third requirement is the one most often overlooked, and the most damaging
to omit. If raters are allocated in fixed pairs — A always with B, C always with
D — then a high agreement figure is confounded with pair identity. Two raters
who share a misreading of the rubric will agree with each other consistently,
and that consistency is indistinguishable from accuracy. Dispersing pairings
means every rater's judgements are repeatedly checked against a *different*
person, so a shared idiosyncrasy is far more likely to surface as disagreement
somewhere.

Sorter satisfies all three, and reports how closely it did so against what is
mathematically attainable for the given numbers.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Formal statement

Let `N` be the number of items and `S` the number of raters, `S ≥ 2`. Assign to
each item an unordered pair of distinct raters. Write `d(r)` for the number of
items assigned to rater `r`, and `m(r, r')` for the number of items assigned to
the pair `{r, r'}`.

The objectives are:

- **Balance:** minimise `max d(r) − min d(r)`.
- **Dispersion:** minimise `max m(r, r')`.

There are `P = C(S,2) = S(S−1)/2` distinct pairs available. Two bounds follow
immediately:

- Since `N` items are distributed over at most `P` distinct pairs,
  `max m(r, r') ≥ ⌈N / P⌉`. When `N ≤ P` this is 1 — no pair need ever repeat.
- Since there are `2N` rater-item slots over `S` raters,
  `max d(r) ≥ ⌈2N / S⌉`, so the best attainable balance spread is 1 whenever
  `S ∤ 2N`, and 0 when it divides.

Sorter attains both bounds. Section 4 reports the verification.

### 2.2 Construction

The allocation is built from a **round-robin tournament schedule**, generated by
the *circle method* — the standard construction for scheduling a single
round-robin, in which one position is held fixed and the remainder rotate.
Applied to `S` raters it enumerates all `P` pairs, arranged into rounds such
that **each rater appears at most once per round**. When `S` is odd a dummy
position is added so that positions pair evenly; the rater opposite it is idle
that round, and over a full schedule each rater is idle exactly once.

Two properties of that ordering do the work:

- **Every pair appears exactly once** across a full schedule, so taking pairs
  in order distributes items across distinct pairings before reusing any.
- **Every rater appears exactly `S−1` times** across a full schedule, so each
  complete pass is perfectly balanced.

The allocation is then simply: walk the schedule in order, cycling back to the
start when it is exhausted, and take `N` pairs.

This yields `max m(r, r') = ⌈N / P⌉` — the lower bound — because pairs are
consumed uniformly, and a balance spread of 0 whenever `N` is a multiple of `P`.

### 2.3 Balance repair

A *partial* final pass is the only source of imbalance. Taking a prefix of the
schedule means some raters have appeared in more of its rounds than others; with
odd `S`, the idle position compounds this. Empirically the spread reaches 2 in
such cases (e.g. `N = 50`, `S = 25`).

A repair pass corrects this. While `max d(r) − min d(r) > 1`, one slot is moved
from the most-loaded rater to the least-loaded. A move is rejected if it would
place the same rater twice on one item, or push any pairing above the `⌈N / P⌉`
bound — so balance is never bought at the cost of dispersion. Each iteration
strictly reduces total imbalance, so the pass terminates.

In testing the constraint-respecting move always existed; the fallback that
would permit one extra repeat never fired.

### 2.4 Position within the pair

Items list a first and a second rater. Position is assigned greedily: of the two
raters on an item, whichever currently holds fewer first positions takes it.
This keeps each rater's first/second split near even (observed within one or two
of an exact half across all tested configurations), so no rater is
systematically in one position — relevant if position carries any procedural
meaning, such as one rater scoring before seeing the other's work.

### 2.5 Reallocation when a rater withdraws

Withdrawal mid-study is handled as a **repair, not a re-run**. Only the slots
held by departing raters are refilled; every remaining rater keeps every item
they already had.

This is a deliberate methodological choice. A full re-allocation would produce a
better-conditioned design on paper, but it invalidates work already completed
and changes the allocation of raters who did nothing wrong — introducing exactly
the kind of mid-study protocol change that is hard to describe honestly in a
methods section. Refilling vacated slots leaves the completed portion of the
design intact and confines the change to what actually had to change.

Each vacated slot is filled by the remaining rater who: (1) is not already on
that item, (2) has the fewest existing shared items with the surviving partner,
and (3) carries the lightest load — in that priority order.

A second pass then rebalances **only among the newly-filled slots**. This was
necessary: the greedy fill alone left a spread of 2 in roughly 5% of tested
scenarios. Because it moves only slots that were vacant anyway, no remaining
rater's existing allocation is disturbed.

Slots marked complete are never reassigned, even when held by a departing rater
— that work exists and reassigning it would discard real data.

## 3. Outputs

Each run writes a timestamped directory containing:

| File | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `assignments.csv` | The allocation |
| `assignments.xlsx` | The same, as a workbook |
| `summary.txt` | Per-rater loads, first/second split, pairing matrix, and attained-versus-optimal figures |
| `run_info.txt` | Parameters and results of the run |

A reallocation run additionally writes `changes.csv` — every slot that moved,
with its previous and new rater — and never modifies the source.

The allocation table is:

| Project ID | First Scorer | Second Scorer | First Scorer Done | Second Scorer Done |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Raters are integer IDs. The tool never handles names, so an allocation can be
published or shared without carrying identifying information; the mapping from
ID to person is kept by the researcher.

`summary.txt` is the file to consult when reporting. It states the attained
figures beside the theoretical optima, for example:

```
Highest load: 12   Lowest load: 11   Difference: 1
  OK    Evenly split — no scorer carries more than one project above another.

Most times any two scorers share a project: 2
Best achievable for these numbers:          2
  OK    Pairings are as spread out as the numbers allow.
```

## 4. Verification

The allocation procedure was checked exhaustively over a grid rather than spot
tested.

**Allocation** — 585 combinations, `S` from 2 to 40 and `N` from 1 to 1000. In
every combination:

- no item received the same rater twice;
- `max d(r) − min d(r) ≤ 1`;
- `max m(r, r')` equalled `⌈N / C(S,2)⌉`, the lower bound.

**Reallocation** — 210 scenarios spanning 3–30 raters, 5–300 items, and 1 to
`S−2` simultaneous withdrawals. In every scenario:

- no departing rater remained anywhere in the allocation;
- no item received the same rater twice;
- **no remaining rater's existing allocation changed**;
- `max d(r) − min d(r) ≤ 1` across remaining raters;
- the change log matched the actual differences exactly.

**End-to-end** — nine representative configurations were run through the full
scripts and the written files re-read and re-verified independently of the code
that produced them, including confirmation that the `.xlsx` and `.csv` agree.

A methods section can reasonably state: *raters were allocated to items by a
round-robin (circle-method) construction with a balance-repair pass, which
attained the minimum achievable pairing repetition and a workload difference of
at most one item across raters.*

## 5. Architecture

```
Sorter/
  assign.py            entry point: asks, computes, writes
  reassign.py          entry point: reads a sheet, refills vacated slots
  sorter/
    algorithm.py       circle method, balance repair, orientation, reallocation
    sheets.py          CSV writer + dependency-free .xlsx writer
    sheet_reader.py    reads a prior sheet back (CSV or .xlsx)
    summary.py         loads, pairing matrix, attained-vs-optimal report
    console.py         prompts, status output, unique run directories
    selfcheck.py       preflight run by the launchers
  Start Main Assign.{command,sh,bat}
  Start Reassign.{command,sh,bat}
  output/              timestamped run directories
```

`algorithm.py` is pure: it takes and returns plain integers and lists, touches
no files, and holds no state. That is what made the exhaustive verification in
section 4 straightforward to run, and it is the module to read first.

### Design decisions

**No third-party dependencies, including for Excel output.** An `.xlsx` file is
a ZIP archive of XML parts; since these sheets are headers and integers, the
workbook is written directly with the standard library rather than pulling in a
spreadsheet package. The tool therefore runs on any machine with Python 3.9+,
offline, with no installation step — which matters for a research instrument
that may need to run on a managed or air-gapped machine, and which keeps the
software environment trivially reproducible.

**Self-contained and location-independent.** Every path resolves relative to the
script, so the folder can be copied anywhere and run. Nothing references the
project it was originally written alongside.

**Timestamped, never overwritten.** Every run writes a new directory, suffixed
if two runs land in the same second. Reallocation never modifies its source. The
allocation history of a study is therefore preserved by construction, not by
discipline.

**Preflight check.** The launchers verify the Python version, the presence of
each standard-library module used, the completeness of the folder, and that the
output directory is writable — each with a specific message — before any work
starts.

**Failures are explained, not raised.** Both entry points catch exceptions and
print a message with the corrective action, then hold the window open. A
double-clicked tool that vanishes on error is unusable to a non-technical
operator.

## 6. Limitations

- **Two raters per item** is fixed. Three or more would need a different
  construction (a balanced incomplete block design).
- **No rater-specific constraints.** Availability, expertise, conflicts of
  interest, and prior exposure to particular items are not modelled. Where those
  matter, treat the output as a starting allocation to adjust.
- **`S = 2` is degenerate** — both raters score everything, and no dispersion is
  possible. The tool proceeds and says so.
- **Reallocation preserves the existing allocation by design**, so after several
  successive withdrawals the dispersion of pairings will drift below what a
  fresh allocation would achieve. `summary.txt` reports the attained figure after
  every reallocation, so the drift is visible. If it becomes material, a fresh
  allocation of the unscored remainder is the remedy.
- **Deterministic.** The same inputs always produce the same allocation. This is
  intended — it makes a study's allocation reproducible from its parameters —
  but it means the allocation is not randomised. If the design calls for random
  assignment, shuffle the mapping from rater IDs to people, which achieves
  randomisation without disturbing the combinatorial properties.
